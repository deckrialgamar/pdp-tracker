Where the GDPR standsGDPR Article 5ported from the GDPR

What the GDPR provides. GDPR Article 5(1) sets the processing principles. Point (a) requires processing to be lawful, fair and transparent in relation to the data subject, point (b) requires collection for specified, explicit and legitimate purposes, and point (c) limits the data to what is adequate, relevant and necessary for those purposes. Article 5(2) then makes the controller responsible for compliance and able to demonstrate it, which is the accountability principle.

The four adjectives in Pasal 27 line up one for one with the GDPR terms. Terbatas answers to Article 5(1)(c), spesifik to the specified purposes in Article 5(1)(b), and sah secara hukum and transparan to the lawfulness and transparency limbs of Article 5(1)(a).

What is absent from the article is the remaining limb of Article 5(1)(a), fairness, though it is not absent from the regime: PP Pasal 60 ayat (2) requires transparent processing to be carried out secara jujur dan iktikad baik, and PP Pasal 35 ayat (3) forbids asking for consent by deceptive or misleading means, which is the direct answer to the interfaces the deceptive design guidelines describe.

The difference is rank rather than presence. In the EU fairness is a principle in Article 5(1)(a) that reaches every basis and every stage; in Indonesia it arrives as a qualifier on transparency and on consent gathering. Article 5(2) accountability has no home in Pasal 27 either, and the nearest Indonesian equivalent is not the recording duty at Pasal 31 but UU Pasal 16 ayat (2) huruf h, which requires processing to be bertanggung jawab dan dapat dibuktikan secara jelas.

Minimisation is where the EU line has moved most: C-446/21 held that Article 5(1)(c) precludes data being aggregated, analysed and processed for targeted advertising without restriction as to time and without distinction as to type of data, and C-439/19 shows the same principles limiting a public register even where a statute authorises the disclosure.

PP Pasal 59 makes that argument operative in Indonesia, since it defines terbatas dan spesifik as minimisation and sets three tests, relationship to the purpose, sufficiency for the purpose, and no excess beyond what the purpose needs.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Articles 5, 6, 89aligned

What the GDPR provides. Purpose limitation in GDPR Article 5(1)(b) has two halves. Data must be collected for specified, explicit and legitimate purposes and must not be further processed in a manner incompatible with them, and the same provision deems further processing for archiving in the public interest, scientific or historical research or statistical purposes not incompatible, where the safeguards in GDPR Article 89(1) are in place.

GDPR Article 6(4) supplies the compatibility test where the reuse rests neither on the data subject's consent nor on a Union or member state law of the kind it describes, weighing the link between the purposes, the context of collection, the nature of the data, the consequences for the subject, and safeguards such as encryption or pseudonymisation.

UU Pasal 28 carries only the first half of the idea. The second half, the bar on further processing incompatible with the original purpose, has no Indonesian counterpart at either level.

PP Pasal 68 is a documentation duty, requiring the purpose and its changes to be recorded and stated in the internal policy and the privacy notice, and PP Pasal 67 ayat (4) sends a controller with an additional or new purpose back to the requirements of the regulation itself rather than to a compatibility test.

So whether analytics or model training can ride on an existing purpose has no operative test in Indonesia, where the GDPR answers it inside Article 6(4). Indonesia adds something the GDPR does not carry: PP Pasal 67 ayat (2) huruf b requires the purpose to be set against the balance of the data subject's interests, which pulls a weighing exercise into purpose specification.

On the EU side the compatibility route is narrower than it looks, but not because of the collection basis. GDPR Article 6(4) opens where the further processing is not based on consent or on a qualifying Union or member state law, and Recital 50 confirms that consent to the new purpose removes the need for any compatibility assessment.

The constraint on a controller that collected on consent comes from the consent rules instead: EDPB Guidelines 05/2020 paragraph 58 requires additional consent for a new purpose unless another basis better reflects the situation, and paragraph 123 forbids swapping out of consent.

Because consent is the dominant basis in Indonesian practice under Pasal 20, that limit will bite hard on secondary use if the PP is read the same way, and C-175/20 shows how tightly the Court holds a public authority collecting data afresh to the specific purposes stated in its own request and to the period strictly necessary.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Articles 5, 16diverges

What the GDPR provides. GDPR Article 5(1)(d) requires personal data to be accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date. The duty is framed as an obligation of means: every reasonable step must be taken to erase or rectify inaccurate data without delay, having regard to the purposes for which it is processed. There is no standalone duty to verify, and completeness and consistency are not named as separate qualities.

Article 16 supplies the subject-driven counterpart, including completion of incomplete data by a supplementary statement.

Article 5(1)(d) is purpose relative, so the reasonable steps to erase or rectify are measured against what the data is used for. C-434/16 is the clearest statement of that logic, holding that a candidate's written answers at a professional examination are personal data whose accuracy is assessed for what the record is, not for whether the answers were correct.

UU Pasal 29 ayat (1) carries no such qualifier and adds kelengkapan and konsistensi as duties in their own right, and ayat (2) converts a standard into a process by making verification freestanding. That inverts the burden of design: an EU controller argues that no reasonable step was called for here, while an Indonesian controller has to show the verification happened, and UU Pasal 57 ayat (1) lists Pasal 29 among the provisions carrying administrative sanction.

The benchmark is not blank. PP Pasal 69 ayat (3) makes the controller fix the mechanism or data quality standard first, ayat (5) sets a floor of four things every verification must reach, the truth or trustworthiness of the data, its completeness against the purpose, uniformity of form or format, and how often verification is needed, ayat (4) makes it periodic or ad hoc, and ayat (8) requires the result to be documented.

What ayat (9) defers to a Peraturan Lembaga is implementation, so the open question is how a regulator will test a controller's own standard, not what the duty covers.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Articles 12, 16, 19diverges

What the GDPR provides. GDPR Article 16 gives the data subject a right to have inaccurate data rectified without undue delay and incomplete data completed, including by a supplementary statement. Article 12(3) sets the outer clock: the controller must tell the subject what action it took within one month of receiving the request, extendable by two further months for complex or numerous requests provided the subject is told of the extension and the reasons within the first month.

Article 12(6) lets the controller ask for further information first where it has reasonable doubts about the requester's identity. Article 19 then requires the correction to be passed to each recipient of the data unless that proves impossible or disproportionate, and the recipients to be named to the subject on request.

The gap is not only the length of the clock but what has to fit inside it.

Under GDPR Article 12(3) the month absorbs identity checking under GDPR Article 12(6), the assessment of whether the data really is inaccurate, and the reply, and how contested that middle step becomes is visible in C-460/20, where the Court held that a search engine operator answering a de-referencing request cannot be required to investigate the facts and that the requester must establish the manifest inaccuracy of the content.

UU Pasal 30 leaves the same work inside 3 x 24 jam with no extension mechanism, where GDPR Article 12(3) allows two further months for complex or numerous requests, and with no identity check provision of the Article 12(6) kind.

C-434/16 marks the cost of a fast clock, since rectification reaches the record and not the substance of what was recorded, so a controller that simply complies alters something it was never obliged to alter. The assessment step itself is not missing on the Indonesian side.

PP Pasal 71 ayat (2) requires the controller to verify the exercise of the right against the purpose of processing, ayat (3) makes replacement follow that verification, ayat (5) allows refusal, ayat (7) requires the refusal and its reasons to be notified, and ayat (4) allows a supplementary note where the original data cannot be replaced, which answers the supplementary statement in GDPR Article 16.

Downstream the two regimes end up close. UU Pasal 30 ayat (2) stops at telling the subject, but PP Pasal 72 ayat (1) carries the correction on to other controllers and processors and ayat (2) carries the same escape as GDPR Article 19 where transmission is impossible or involves disproportionate effort. What the PP does not carry is the second half of Article 19, the right of the data subject to be told who those recipients were.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Article 30diverges

What the GDPR provides. GDPR Article 30 requires a written register, not a log. A controller records the purposes of processing, the categories of data subjects and of personal data, the categories of recipients, transfers to third countries with the safeguards relied on, the envisaged erasure time limits where possible, and a general description of the security measures.

Article 30(2) sets a shorter list for processors, and Article 30(4) makes the register available to the supervisory authority on request. Article 30(5) exempts an organisation employing fewer than 250 persons unless the processing is likely to risk rights and freedoms, is other than occasional, or covers special category or criminal conviction data.

Two differences run here, and the exemption is only the visible one. GDPR Article 30(5) lets an enterprise or organisation employing fewer than 250 persons keep no register, but the relief falls away where the processing is not occasional, where it is likely to result in a risk to rights and freedoms, or where it covers special category or criminal conviction data, and it lifts the register duty alone rather than anything else in the Regulation.

The Indonesian text grants no relief at any size. The second difference is what is being recorded. GDPR Article 30 is an inventory of processing operations, written once and kept current, whereas perekaman terhadap seluruh kegiatan pemrosesan reads naturally as capturing every processing event, which is a duty of a different order and cost.

PP Pasal 74 ayat (2) settles that as an inventory of thirteen fields, and huruf k already carries Masa Retensi, which is the envisaged erasure time limit that GDPR Article 30(1)(f) folds into the register, with PP Pasal 75 adding a separate retention policy document on top.

The role split survives, but by a different route: GDPR Article 30(2) binds the processor in its own name and GDPR Article 30(4) puts the register in front of the supervisory authority on request, while PP Pasal 74 ayat (3) and ayat (5) make the Pengendali ensure the Prosesor records and prescribe the shorter processor list, so the processor duty runs through the controller rather than directly.

Both provisions give access to the data and to something about how it was handled, but the second half was defined very differently.

Article 15 lists metadata and its edges had to be litigated: C-154/21 turned categories of recipients into the actual identity of recipients where the subject asks, C-487/21 made a copy mean a faithful and intelligible reproduction rather than a summary, and C-579/21 held that consultation logs fall inside the right while the identity of the employees who consulted does not, save where that identity is essential to the exercise of the subject's rights.

UU Pasal 32 ayat (1) starts roughly where the EU arrived, with rekam jejak pemrosesan named as an object of the right and bounded by the retention period rather than by a list of fields. The limits then sit in a different place and cut harder. GDPR Article 15(4) keeps the third party ceiling inside the access right, so the controller trims what it releases and hands over the rest.

UU Pasal 32 carries no limit of its own, and the third party question is answered one article later at UU Pasal 33 huruf b, where disclosure of another person's data obliges the controller to refuse. The C-579/21 outcome, log released and names withheld, therefore has no obvious route in the Indonesian text, because the ground that narrows the answer in the EU defeats the request in Indonesia.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Articles 12, 15, 23diverges

What the GDPR provides. The GDPR does not oblige a controller to refuse an access request. It limits the right instead, and it does so in two different ways. GDPR Article 15(4) is a ceiling that binds the controller directly: the right to obtain a copy shall not adversely affect the rights and freedoms of others, so the controller must hold back the part that would.

GDPR Article 23 is a permission addressed to the legislator, letting Union or member state law restrict Articles 12 to 22, Article 34 and the corresponding parts of Article 5 for a closed list of objectives, national security and defence among them, but only where the restriction respects the essence of the right and is necessary and proportionate.

GDPR Article 23(2) then fixes what such a law must itself contain where relevant, including the scope of the restriction, the safeguards against abuse, and the subject's right to be told a restriction applies unless telling would defeat its purpose. GDPR Article 12(4) requires the controller to tell the subject without delay and at the latest within one month why it is not acting, and to point to the supervisory authority and to judicial remedy.

The mandatory limb is the difference. UU Pasal 33 says wajib menolak, so a controller that grants access on one of the three grounds is itself in breach. The GDPR has no duty to refuse a request as such.

Article 15(4) obliges the controller to hold back only the part of a copy that would harm others, and Article 23 is a permission addressed to the legislator, not to the controller.

The route differs as much as the obligation: under GDPR Article 23 a restriction has to be created by a legislative measure carrying the Article 23(2) content list, so the controller applies a rule someone else drafted and that can be tested for necessity and proportionality, whereas UU Pasal 33 puts the three grounds in the statute and hands the assessment to the controller with no proportionality test on its face.

Limb b, disclosure of another person's data, is the limb the EU has actually litigated: C-487/21 reads GDPR Article 15(4) as requiring, wherever possible, a means of communicating the data that does not infringe the rights of others, and C-579/21 lets employee identities be withheld from log data while the log data itself is handed over, so third party data narrows what must be produced rather than defeating the request.

The national security limb has no controller side counterpart at all, because GDPR Article 23 keeps that judgement with the legislator and leaves it reviewable.

The remedy side does not close the gap either: PP Pasal 26 leaves the conditions of refusal to the controller under other legislation and PP Pasal 27 gives the subject a route to report to the Lembaga, but neither carries the GDPR Article 12(4) duty to give reasons on a one month clock or to point to a judicial remedy.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Articles 5, 28, 29, 32, 90diverges

What the GDPR provides. The GDPR has no free-standing duty of confidentiality resting on the controller. GDPR Article 5(1)(f) pairs confidentiality with integrity and states it as a security principle, protection against unauthorised or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction or damage, by appropriate technical and organisational measures.

Secrecy as such is handled by other instruments: GDPR Article 28(3)(b) requires the processor contract to bind the processor's authorised persons to confidentiality or to an existing statutory secrecy duty, and GDPR Article 29 with Article 32(4) stops anyone acting under the controller's or the processor's authority from processing except on instructions.

GDPR Article 90 creates no secrecy duty of its own; it lets member states set out how far a supervisory authority's powers under Article 58(1)(e) and (f) reach into controllers already bound by professional secrecy.

The two provisions do different jobs behind similar words. UU Pasal 36 is a secrecy duty, keep the data confidential, stated once and owed by the Pengendali. GDPR Article 5(1)(f) is a security duty that happens to use the word confidentiality, requiring appropriate security of the data, and it is read with GDPR Article 32 rather than as an independent obligation of silence.

The GDPR's secrecy obligations reach natural persons through instruments and not directly, by contract at GDPR Article 28(3)(b) for a processor's authorised persons and by instruction at GDPR Article 29 and Article 32(4) for anyone acting under the controller's authority. That gives the two systems different failure points.

In the EU a controller that took no steps to bind its own staff to instructions breaches Article 32(4), while the leaking employee ordinarily answers to national law rather than as a controller, though Guidelines 07/2020 accept that a person who processes for their own purposes outside the organisation's activities can become a controller in their own name.

Under UU Pasal 36 the duty and the sanction land on the Pengendali whether or not any instrument exists, and PP Pasal 125 ayat (2) then folds the processor in by making the controller ensure the Prosesor can support the same confidentiality, which is the work of GDPR Article 28(3)(b) done without a contract term.

The consequence is that an EU controller answers first for the instruments it put in place, and an Indonesian one answers for the confidentiality whether or not it put any instrument in place at all.

What the GDPR provides. The GDPR imposes no free-standing duty of supervision. GDPR Article 28 attaches to a moment and to an instrument. What the GDPR provides. The GDPR splits unlawful processing in two. UU Pasal 38 does not say whose unlawful processing is being guarded against, and that silence carries the article. The tracker places it with PP Pasal 129 beside the security provisions, which resolves it the first way, and the resolution matters because the two produce different defences. A security failure is judged by whether the measures were appropriate.

A lawfulness failure is judged by whether a basis applied, which is a narrower argument, though not a closed one, since GDPR Article 6(1)(f) turns on a balancing of interests and Article 6(4) on a compatibility test.

The GDPR keeps the two apart on purpose, which is why Article 5(1)(f) has Article 32 to say how the duty is discharged while Article 6 has no equivalent article of measures, only the conditions at Articles 6(2) to (4), 7, 8 and 9.

C-340/21 shows the appropriateness route running, with the burden on the controller to prove its measures were adequate and an intrusion not proving by itself that they were not, and C-807/21 shows how far responsibility for other people's unlawful acts reaches, to any person acting in the course of the controller's business and on the controller's behalf.

The second asymmetry is at the back end, where GDPR Article 32 is joined to Articles 33 and 34, so a failure of prevention is also a personal data breach under Article 4(12) and puts the controller on a clock that starts when it becomes aware, subject to the risk threshold at Article 33(1), while UU Pasal 39 stops at prevention and leaves the consequence to the notification provisions elsewhere.

C-340/21 is where both halves met, an unauthorised access at a tax authority, with the Court holding that the fact of the intrusion does not by itself prove the Article 32 measures were inadequate and that the burden of proving they were appropriate sits on the controller.

What the GDPR provides. Article 5(1)(b) requires the purposes to be specified at collection and bars further processing incompatible with them, compatible further processing being permitted and tested under Article 6(4).

Article 17(1)(a) turns the same idea into a right once the data are no longer necessary, and Article 7(3) stops future consent based processing on withdrawal. There is no GDPR article that says processing must end; the end is an effect of the principles plus the erasure right.

Both regimes stop processing at the same three points, but the GDPR never states the duty, it derives it. That changes what an enforcer has to prove, and C-77/21 shows how narrow the finding then is.

The Court held that purpose limitation does not preclude copying data into a test database where the further processing is compatible with the purposes of the original collection, compatibility being tested against the Article 6(4) criteria, and it located the infringement in Article 5(1)(e) alone, for keeping the copy longer than the tests required.

UU Pasal 42 ayat (1) is an express wajib with a named trigger, so nothing has to be reconstructed from principle. Article 5(1)(e) fixes no period at all, and what makes it auditable is the disclosure duty at Article 13(2)(a) and the envisaged erasure time limits recorded under Article 30(1)(f), while Pasal 42 ayat (1) huruf a points at a masa retensi that PP Pasal 75 turns into a standing policy document, a heavier artefact than anything the GDPR names.

The third trigger is the real divergence: Pasal 42 ayat (1) huruf c reads as an unqualified subject request, whereas GDPR request grounds are closed at Article 17(1) and cut down further by Article 17(3), so the Indonesian text is broader on its face and narrowed only by the reference back to legislation at ayat (2).

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Articles 7, 17ported from the GDPR

What the GDPR provides. GDPR Article 17(1) gives a right to erasure without undue delay on six grounds, among them that the data are no longer necessary for the purposes, that consent has been withdrawn and no other basis applies, and that the data have been unlawfully processed. Article 17(2) adds that a controller who made the data public must take reasonable steps to inform other controllers of the request.

Article 17(3) then removes the right where processing is needed for freedom of expression, a legal obligation, public health, archiving under Article 89(1), or legal claims. Article 7(3) supplies the withdrawal mechanism that feeds the second ground.

The limbs track Article 17(1) and in its order. Pasal 43 ayat (1) huruf a is Article 17(1)(a), huruf b is Article 17(1)(b) read with Article 7(3), and huruf d is Article 17(1)(d), the omitted grounds being the objection route at Article 17(1)(c), the legal obligation route at (e) and the child and information society route at (f).

One limb was widened in transit: Article 17(1)(b) erases only where consent is withdrawn and no other legal ground applies, while huruf b states bare withdrawal, so a controller holding a second basis keeps the data in Europe and loses that argument on the face of the Indonesian text. What was not carried across matters more.

The statute has no counterpart to the Article 17(3) balance with free expression, public health or archiving. The exceptions that do reach Pasal 43 are the four state interests at UU Pasal 50 ayat (1), which cover huruf a to huruf c only and overlap Article 17(3) at nothing wider than the public task limb of Article 17(3)(b), so the rest of that balance sits a tier lower, at PP Pasal 83.

Article 17(2) has no Indonesian counterpart either, and C-129/21 shows what that costs: the Court held that Article 17(2) does not preclude a supervisory authority ordering a directory provider to take reasonable steps to inform search engine providers of a withdrawal, and it grounded the parallel duty to inform the originating operator and other directory providers in Article 5(2) and Article 24.

Note finally the drafting effect of huruf c: naming a subject request as one trigger among four leaves the other three as duties the controller runs on its own initiative, whereas Article 17(1) is built as a right the subject exercises and the search engine practice in C-131/12 and C-460/20 assumes a claimant who asks.

What the GDPR provides. The GDPR has no destruction duty distinct from erasure. It uses the word destruction in Article 4(2), where destruction is one form of processing, and in Article 4(12), Article 5(1)(f) and Article 32, where it is something a breach does to data.

Disposal in the Regulation runs on three provisions: the storage limitation principle at Article 5(1)(e), the erasure obligation at Article 17, and the processor's duty at Article 28(3)(g) to delete or return the data at the end of the service. The Regulation prescribes no method, so deletion, overwriting and irreversible anonymisation are each capable of discharging it.

Article 17(3)(b), (d) and (e) hold data back where they are needed for a legal obligation, for archiving in the public interest under Article 89(1), or for legal claims, and Article 89(3) lets Union or member state law derogate from the rights in Articles 15, 16, 18, 19, 20 and 21 for archiving in the public interest.

Three of the four Pasal 44 triggers are GDPR erasure grounds wearing another name: huruf a is Article 5(1)(e) with Article 17(1)(a), huruf b is a request under Article 17(1), and huruf d is Article 17(1)(d). What diverges is the architecture and one trigger.

Europe runs a single disposal track, principle at Article 5(1)(e), obligation and right at Article 17, processor clean up at Article 28(3)(g), while Indonesia runs penghapusan at Pasal 43 and pemusnahan at Pasal 44 as separate wajib with separate sanctions, so the destruction duty and its sanction at PP Pasal 84(1) have nowhere to land in a control set derived from GDPR mappings.

Pasal 44 ayat (1) huruf c is the trigger with no European shape at all. The limbs are joined by dan/atau, so on its face the absence of a link to penyelesaian proses hukum can start the duty on its own, a reading that cannot be intended and is better treated as an open drafting question.

Read either way it runs against Article 17(3)(e), which makes the presence of a legal claim a shield against erasure, so the same diligence output is a defence in Europe and a condition on the Indonesian side. The jadwal retensi arsip hook at huruf a ties the trigger to Indonesian archives law, and the GDPR supplies no answer of its own, because Article 89 gives safeguards and a derogation power for archiving rather than an archive schedule.

One design consequence follows: anonymisation can discharge Article 17 because the GDPR test is identifiability, whereas Pasal 44 is keyed to the archive schedule and to lawfulness of acquisition, so one technical measure does not necessarily close both.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Articles 12, 17, 19diverges

What the GDPR provides. GDPR Article 19 requires the controller to communicate any rectification, erasure or restriction carried out under Article 16, Article 17(1) or Article 18 to each recipient to whom the data were disclosed, unless that proves impossible or involves disproportionate effort. Its second sentence reaches the data subject, but only on request, and only to name those recipients.

Article 17(2) sits beside it for published data, requiring reasonable steps, including technical measures, to inform other controllers of the erasure request. The subject learns what was done through Article 12(3), which requires information on action taken within one month of a request.

The addressee is inverted on both sides. Article 19 points at recipients by default and reaches the subject only if the subject asks, and only to name those recipients, while UU Pasal 45 points at the subject unconditionally and says nothing about recipients.

The GDPR has no free standing duty to confirm to the subject that erasure has happened, because that confirmation arrives through the request handling machinery at Article 12(3), which means it does not arise where the controller erased on its own initiative. Pasal 45 does arise there, since it attaches to any penghapusan or pemusnahan including the controller initiated triggers at Pasal 43 ayat (1) huruf a and Pasal 44.

In the other direction the Indonesian propagation duty stops at the processor, because PP Pasal 88 is a flow down to processors, while the Article 19 recipient, defined at Article 4(9), takes in independent controllers downstream.

How far the European chain runs is set by more than Article 19: in C-129/21 the Court grounded the duty to inform the originating operator and other directory providers in Article 5(2) and Article 24, and held that Article 17(2) does not preclude an authority ordering reasonable steps to inform search engine providers. So the two regimes are strong at opposite ends, Indonesia on proving the act to the subject, the GDPR on chasing the data through the chain.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Articles 32, 33, 34diverges

What the GDPR provides. GDPR Article 33(1) requires notification to the supervisory authority without undue delay and where feasible within 72 hours of the controller becoming aware of a breach, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to rights and freedoms.

Article 33(3) fixes the content, including the categories and approximate numbers of subjects and records, the likely consequences and the measures taken, and Article 33(4) allows the information to be supplied in phases.

Article 34(1) reaches the data subject only where the breach is likely to result in a high risk, and Article 34(3) removes even that where the data were encrypted, where the risk has since been contained, or where individual contact would take disproportionate effort, in which case a public communication substitutes. Article 33(5) requires the controller to document every breach, notified or not.

The clock is the difference that gets missed, and it is a difference about the start, not the length.

Article 33(1) runs 72 hours from the controller becoming aware, and Guidelines 9/2022 devotes a section to when awareness begins, whereas UU Pasal 46 ayat (1) fixes 3 x 24 hours against the occurrence of the kegagalan and omits the terhitung sejak formula that Pasal 41 ayat (1) uses for the restriction clock, so a controller that spends two days confirming an incident cannot tell from the statute whether it is early or already late.

PP Pasal 114 ayat (2) supplies the start the statute omits, counting from when the failure is known with certainty, properly and reasonably, and its elucidation ties that knowledge to the conclusion of the controller's own incident documentation.

Then the filters, which Indonesia does not have on either limb: the GDPR notifies the authority unless risk is unlikely and the subject only on high risk, so Pasal 46 produces notices in cases where an EU controller would document and stay silent.

The public notice is not the convergence it looks like either, because Article 34(3)(c) offers a public communication as a substitute for individual notice, while Pasal 46 ayat (3) adds one on top of the individual notice that ayat (1) huruf a already requires.

The sharpest inversion is evidential, because Article 33(5) makes the internal register of all breaches directly enforceable and fineable under Article 83(4)(a), which is how a supervisory authority audits the risk judgment behind a decision not to notify, and C-340/21 adds the litigation half, holding that in an Article 82 damages claim the controller bears the burden of proving its Article 32 measures were appropriate.

The tracker records PP Pasal 116 as neither wajib nor listed at Pasal 184(1), so Indonesia removed the discretion and also removed the record that would have tested it, which is a coherent design only for as long as no one asks what happened before the notice went out.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Articles 5, 24, 30ported from the GDPR

What the GDPR provides. GDPR Article 5(2) makes the controller responsible for the Article 5(1) principles and requires it to be able to demonstrate compliance with them, a pairing the Regulation itself names accountability. Article 24(1) turns that into a duty to implement technical and organisational measures appropriate to the risk and to review and update them.

Article 24(2) requires data protection policies where proportionate, and Article 24(3) allows adherence to a code of conduct or a certification to be used as an element of the demonstration. The artefacts that carry the proof are elsewhere: records under Article 30, security under Article 32, impact assessments under Article 35.

Pasal 47 pairs responsibility with the ability to show it, in the same order as Article 5(2). The objects are not identical. Article 5(2) keys both limbs to the Article 5(1) principles, while Pasal 47 makes the controller responsible for pemrosesan Data Pribadi and keys only the demonstration limb to the principles, so the first limb reaches wider than Article 5(2) and sits closer to Article 24(1), whose object is compliance with the Regulation entire.

What did not travel is the evidence catalogue. Article 30 records, Article 32 measures and Article 35 assessments are each independently enforceable and each fineable under Article 83(4)(a), which is what converts Article 5(2) from a statement of posture into a file a regulator can open without proving harm to anyone.

PP Pasal 28 and Pasal 138 rebuild part of that, but the list of things a controller must be able to produce is shorter, so the demonstrability duty in Indonesia points at fewer named documents than its European source does.

One question the Indonesian text leaves open has been answered in Europe: C-807/21 holds that a fine may not be made conditional on prior attribution of the infringement to an identified natural person, and that a fine may be imposed only where the controller acted intentionally or negligently, so accountability there is fault based rather than strict, and C-683/21 says the same about fault while settling who counts as the controller bearing it.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Articles 6, 14diverges

What the GDPR provides. The GDPR has no article on mergers, demergers, acquisitions, consolidations or dissolution. A database moving in a transaction falls back on the ordinary tools: the acquirer needs a legal basis under Article 6(1), a compatibility test under Article 6(4) if the purpose shifts, and Article 14 information to the subject because the data came from a source other than the subject, at the latest within one month under Article 14(3)(a).

Article 13(3) catches any controller that collected from the subject and then processes for a new purpose, which is how the seller side is reached. Corporate succession is left to national company law, and the Regulation says nothing about winding up.

The GDPR reaches the notification limb through two general duties rather than a dedicated one. The acquirer owes Article 14 information because it obtained the data from a source other than the subject, discharged once and after the event, at the latest within one month under Article 14(3)(a).

The seller is reached by Article 13(3) where the transfer is a purpose other than the one it collected for, and that information is owed before the further processing begins. So Europe does produce a notice on each side of the transaction, but split across two controllers and two provisions and triggered by a purpose change, where Pasal 48 ayat (2) puts both notices on the transferring entity and triggers them off the corporate act itself.

Dissolution is the wider gap. Article 5(1)(e) and Article 17 still apply to a company being wound up, but the Regulation never says who performs the deletion or what becomes of the data when the controller ceases to exist, whereas Pasal 48 ayat (3) and ayat (4) put storage, transfer, erasure and destruction on the dissolution itself and require the subject to be told.

The exit an acquirer reaches for in Europe is narrower than it looks, because Article 14(5)(b) is read by the transparency guidelines as tied to archiving, research and statistical purposes and not to be relied on routinely outside them, with the number of data subjects only one factor in the balance, so it is a weak defence in Europe and no defence at all under Pasal 48, which UU Pasal 50 does not list among the exempted duties.

The practical consequence for diligence is that an EU style checklist hangs the seller's notice off a purpose change, so a pre closing notice tied to the corporate act, as at PP Pasal 133, has no line to sit on and gets found after signing.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Articles 2, 23diverges

What the GDPR provides. GDPR Article 23 lets Union or member state law restrict the scope of the obligations and rights in Articles 12 to 22, Article 34 and the corresponding parts of Article 5.

The restriction must come by legislative measure, must respect the essence of fundamental rights and freedoms, and must be necessary and proportionate in a democratic society to safeguard one of the listed objectives, among them national security, defence, public security, criminal enforcement and other important objectives of general public interest.

Article 23(2) then dictates what that measure must contain: purposes, categories of data, scope of the restriction, safeguards against abuse, the controller, storage periods, risks to data subjects, and whether data subjects are told about the restriction. A restriction cannot be inferred, and nothing in Article 23 lets an administrative practice stand in for the legislative measure.

Only one of the four interests in UU Pasal 50 ayat (1) is handled in the EU by scope rather than by exception: processing by competent authorities for criminal law purposes is carved out by Article 2(2)(d) and lands in the Law Enforcement Directive with its own rights regime.

National security is not the clean carve-out it looks like, because Article 2(2)(a) removes the state's own activity while a private controller compelled to process for a national security objective stays inside the GDPR, which is why national security also appears as a restriction ground at Article 23(1)(a).

The other two limbs, public interest in the administration of the state and financial sector supervision, are Article 23(1)(e) territory, but Article 23 would still demand a distinct legislative measure carrying the Article 23(2) content list, where UU Pasal 50 ayat (2) asks only that the exception be exercised in the framework of implementing statutory provisions.

Neither the necessity and proportionality test nor the essence test appears in the Indonesian text. The structural difference runs the other way too: Article 23 restricts data subject rights and the matching controller duties in one operation, while UU Pasal 50 lists exempted duties and leaves exceptions to rights in a separate provision, which is why PP Pasal 149 to 159 had to build two parallel sets and why an exception on one side does not carry to the other.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Article 28diverges

What the GDPR provides. GDPR Article 28(1) lets a controller use only processors that provide sufficient guarantees of appropriate technical and organisational measures. Article 28(3) requires a contract or other legal act binding the processor to the controller and fixes what it must contain, starting with processing only on documented instructions.

Article 28(2) bars the processor from engaging another processor without prior written authorisation, specific or general, and where the authorisation is general the processor must give notice of intended changes so the controller can object. Article 28(4) keeps the first processor fully liable to the controller for the sub-processor's performance, and Article 28(10) turns a processor that determines purposes and means into a controller for that processing.

UU Pasal 51 reaches the same allocation of responsibility, then parts company with Article 28 on two mechanisms. Ayat (6) sits where Article 28(10) sits: responsibility moves to the processor once it processes outside the order and the stated purpose, while ayat (3) keeps instructed processing inside the controller's responsibility.

The GDPR gets there by a change of status, which drags the whole controller regime along with it, including the need for the processor to hold its own lawful basis under Article 6(1). UU Pasal 51 ayat (6) shifts responsibility without saying what the processor then is. On sub-processing the two texts split over liability rather than over consent.

Article 28(2) permits a general written authorisation with a right to object, which is what makes multi-tier cloud chains workable in the EU. Neither UU Pasal 51 ayat (5) nor PP Pasal 15 ayat (2) says whether a standing authorisation counts, so that question is open in Indonesia rather than closed against it.

The liability split is real: Article 28(4) keeps the first processor fully liable to the controller for the sub-processor's performance, while PP Pasal 15 ayat (5) puts processing by the sub-processor back on the controller. The chain is not otherwise unguarded.

UU Pasal 37, applied to the processor by UU Pasal 52 and sanction-backed through UU Pasal 57 ayat (1), requires supervision of every party involved in processing under its control, and PP Pasal 15 ayat (3) and ayat (4) require the first processor to ensure the sub-processor complies and to fix an equivalent level of protection by written agreement.

What has no Indonesian counterpart is the selection standard at Article 28(1) and the mandatory contract content at Article 28(3): PP Pasal 15 ayat (4) and PP Pasal 139 ayat (2) require a written agreement but do not fix what it must say.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Articles 28, 29, 30, 32, 33aligned

What the GDPR provides. The GDPR does not switch controller duties onto processors in a single clause. It names the processor inside each article that binds it: Article 29 and Article 28(3)(a) confine it to the controller's documented instructions, Article 30(2) gives it its own record of processing activities, Article 32 imposes security on controller and processor alike, and Article 33(2) requires it to notify the controller of a breach without undue delay.

Those duties bind directly, and Article 83(4)(a) exposes the processor to fines for breaching them regardless of what its contract says. Duties the GDPR keeps with the controller, such as lawful basis, transparency and answering data subject requests, never transfer; the processor only assists under Article 28(3)(e) and (f).

The destination is close and the route is not. UU Pasal 52 is one cross-reference clause switching a fixed block of controller duties onto the processor: accuracy and verification at UU Pasal 29, records of processing at UU Pasal 31, security at UU Pasal 35, confidentiality at UU Pasal 36, supervision of every party involved at UU Pasal 37, and protection against unlawful processing and unauthorised access at UU Pasal 38 and UU Pasal 39.

UU Pasal 57 ayat (1) lists Pasal 52 by number, so the block is sanction-backed and the Indonesian processor answers to the regulator and not only to its customer, which is the same exposure Article 83(4)(a) creates in the EU.

PP Pasal 140 repeats the cross-reference against the PP's own numbering, and PP Pasal 141 adds a free-standing accountability duty covering documentation, responses to the Lembaga, evidence of compliance and audits, which the GDPR asks of the controller under Article 5(2) and Article 24 and not of the processor. Two limbs do not line up.

UU Pasal 29 puts accuracy, completeness, consistency and verification on the processor, where the accuracy principle in the EU stays with the controller. And the block carries no breach duty at all: the counterpart to Article 33(2) is PP Pasal 118, which requires the processor to report a breach to the controller at the first opportunity, sits outside UU Pasal 52, and is absent from the PP Pasal 184 sanctions list.

The article-by-article technique has one property the cross-reference cannot reproduce: the EU list maintains itself, because whether a duty reaches the processor is settled inside the duty itself, where UU Pasal 52 fixes the answer as a list of article numbers that has to be amended whenever the substantive articles move. The other difference is at the boundary of obedience.

The closing subparagraph of Article 28(3), attached to point (h), obliges the processor to tell the controller at once if, in its opinion, an instruction infringes the law, and Article 28(10) states the consequence of going further, so acting on instructions is a defence with a stated limit.

UU Pasal 52 imports duties but not that exchange, and the nearest equivalent sits back at UU Pasal 51 ayat (6), which reallocates responsibility after the event rather than requiring the processor to raise the objection before it acts.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Article 37ported from the GDPR

What the GDPR provides. GDPR Article 37(1) requires a controller or processor to designate a data protection officer in three cases. The first is processing carried out by a public authority or body, courts acting in a judicial capacity excepted. The second is core activities that by their nature, scope or purposes require regular and systematic monitoring of data subjects on a large scale.

The third is core activities consisting of large-scale processing of special categories under Article 9 and of criminal conviction and offence data under Article 10. The three cases are alternatives, and each names the processor as well as the controller. Article 37(5) sets the selection standard: professional qualities, in particular expert knowledge of data protection law and practice, and the ability to fulfil the Article 39 tasks.

Article 37(6) allows a staff member or a service contract, Article 37(2) allows a group of undertakings to share one officer who is easily accessible from each establishment, and Article 37(7) requires the contact details to be published and given to the supervisory authority.

The tracking is limb for limb: UU Pasal 53 ayat (1) huruf b carries the Article 37(1)(b) formula intact, with kegiatan inti and sifat, ruang lingkup, dan/atau tujuan sitting where core activities and nature, scope and/or purposes sit, huruf c carries Article 37(1)(c) down to the criminal offence category, ayat (2) follows Article 37(5) in the same order and ayat (3) is Article 37(6).

Huruf a is the first departure, because it triggers on processing for public services rather than on the identity of a public authority, so it reaches private operators running public service functions that Article 37(1)(a) would not touch, and it drops the exclusion of courts acting judicially.

The chapeau addresses controller and processor together, as Article 37(1) does, but huruf b and huruf c then speak only of kegiatan inti Pengendali Data Pribadi, so on the face of the text a processor whose own core activity is large-scale monitoring escapes the trigger that Article 37(1)(b) and (c) apply to it expressly.

The limbs are also joined by dan where Article 37(1) joins its three cases with or, which on a strict reading would demand all three triggers at once. PP Pasal 142 ayat (1) answers one of these and not the other: huruf b there ends dan/atau, which settles the alternative reading, while huruf b and huruf c still speak only of the controller's core activities, so the processor gap survives into the regulation.

The UU also leaves out Article 37(2) and Article 37(7), so nothing at statute level lets a group share one officer or requires the contact details to reach the authority. One departure runs the other way: Article 37(1)(c) joins special categories and criminal offence data with and, and huruf c writes dan/atau, which settles a reading the EU text leaves open.

Where the GDPR standsGDPR Articles 38, 39ported from the GDPR

What the GDPR provides. GDPR Article 39(1) gives the officer at least five tasks: informing and advising the controller, processor and staff who process; monitoring compliance with the GDPR and with internal policies, including allocation of responsibilities, awareness raising, training and audits; advising on a data protection impact assessment and monitoring its performance under Article 35; cooperating with the supervisory authority; and acting as its contact point, including for prior consultation under Article 36.

Article 39(2) requires the officer to have due regard to the risk of the processing, taking account of its nature, scope, context and purposes. Article 38 is the other half of the design and governs position rather than work.

It requires timely involvement in all data protection issues, resources and access, no instructions on how to perform the tasks, no dismissal or penalty for performing them, direct reporting to the highest management level, secrecy, and no other tasks that create a conflict of interests.

UU Pasal 54 ayat (1) is Article 39(1) in the same order with the last two limbs folded into one: huruf a is Article 39(1)(a), huruf b is Article 39(1)(b), huruf c sits where Article 39(1)(c) sits, and huruf d merges cooperation with the authority and the contact point role from Article 39(1)(d) and (e), with paling sedikit doing the work of at least in the chapeau.

Huruf c is the one limb that shifted in transposition: Article 39(1)(c) has the officer advise where requested and monitor the performance of the impact assessment, while huruf c drops the request condition and has the officer memantau kinerja of the controller and the processor, which widens the task and overlaps huruf b.

Ayat (2) is Article 39(2) carried across, with risiko, sifat, ruang lingkup, konteks and tujuan in the same sequence as risk, nature, scope, context and purposes.

What did not come across into the statute is Article 38, and that is where the EU litigation has actually been: the questions that reached the Court were whether national law may protect the officer against dismissal under Article 38(3) and whether a manager with operational duties can hold the role at all under Article 38(6).

Neither has a hook in UU Pasal 54, but only the first is missing from the regime as a whole. PP Pasal 146 goes well past resources and access.

Ayat (1) huruf a requires the PPDP to be involved in all processing activities, huruf b requires reporting access to the highest management level, huruf c requires the PPDP to operate objectively, free from intervention and independently, and huruf d requires adequate resources and the maintenance of expertise, while ayat (2) requires the controller and processor to ensure the PPDP's tasks do not produce a conflict of interests.

That answers Article 38(1), the no-instructions and direct-reporting limbs of Article 38(3), Article 38(2) and Article 38(6), so the X-FAB question has a text to be argued under. What has no counterpart is the protection against dismissal or penalty for performing the tasks in the second sentence of Article 38(3), and the secrecy duty at Article 38(5).

A PPDP removed after an unwelcome opinion still has no provision addressed to the removal, and PP Pasal 146 is itself absent from the PP Pasal 184 sanctions list, so the independence guarantees that do exist carry no administrative penalty of their own.
